Abstract

Ground-to-space astronomical super-resolution requires recovering space-quality images from ground-based observations that are simultaneously limited by pixel sampling resolution and atmospheric seeing, which imposes a stochastic, spatially varying PSF that cannot be resolved through upsampling alone. Existing methods rely on synthetic training pairs that fail to capture real atmospheric statistics and are prone to either over-smoothed reconstructions or hallucination sources with no physical counterpart in the observed sky. We propose FluxFlow, a conservative pixel-space flow-matching framework that incorporates observation uncertainty and source-region importance weights during training, and a training-free Wiener-regularized test-time correction to suppress hallucination sources while preserving recovered detail. We further construct the DESI–HST Dataset, the large-scale real-world benchmark comprising 19,500 real co-registered ground-to-space image pairs with real atmospheric PSF variation. Experiments demonstrate that FluxFlow consistently outperforms existing baseline methods in both photometric and scientific accuracy. Data and source code will be publicly available.

What is a deep field?

A deep field is a small area of sky observed for long enough to reveal very faint galaxies. Repeated exposures collect light that shorter observations miss.

03The method

One observation. A learned proposal. A check at every step.

FluxFlow turns one low-resolution DESI image into a finer estimate of the same sky. Here is the complete method, followed by the four parts in the video presentation.

01Weighted training

Learn from the reliable sky signal.

Paired DESI and HST images teach the model how fine structure changes across resolution. Pixel weights reduce the influence of unreliable HST pixels, while a source mask gives astronomical sources more weight.

Train once. Reconstruct from DESI.

The HST inverse-variance weight map (WHT) says which target pixels are reliable. The learned conditional U-Net then supplies a velocity field: a direction for updating the current image.

At inference, the input is the DESI observation and a noise state. There is no HST image of the new field to copy.

02Measurement check

Check each proposal against the observation.

Sharper does not always mean faithful: a random fluctuation can grow into a plausible source. Measurement-Consistent Flow Sampling (MC-FS) checks whether a candidate would produce the DESI image we actually observed.

  1. ProposeTake an Euler step with the learned velocity.
  2. ObserveBlur with the point-spread function (PSF), then downsample to the DESI grid.
  3. ComparePredicted minus observed pixels gives the residual.
  4. CorrectLift, Wiener-filter and subtract the residual; repeat for 10 steps.

A proposal can drift toward a false source.

The observation supplies a corrective direction.

The correction is training-free: it does not backpropagate through the network.

03Wiener correction

Make the correction as sharp as the proposal.

The residual must return from the DESI grid to the fine image. Plain PSF back-projection recovers fine spatial frequencies slowly over a finite number of steps. Wiener changes this correction kernel; it does not filter the whole reconstructed image.

04Conditional network

The learned engine behind each Euler step.

A four-level conditional U-Net sees the upsampled DESI image together with the current flow state. Time-conditioned FiLM tells the network where it is in the trajectory; its output is pixel-space velocity.

One role in the larger method.

The network proposes how the image should change. MC-FS checks that proposal against DESI, and Wiener maps the discrepancy back to the fine grid.

The network predicts directly in pixel space, without a VAE. The measurement check and correction stay outside it.

05Reliability

Generative detail can look convincing even when the observation does not support it.

The riskA model can turn a noise fluctuation into a plausible-looking star or galaxy, producing an unreliable source catalogue.

The checkBlur and downsample the reconstruction, then compare it with DESI. MC-FS uses this discrepancy to reduce unsupported structure.

The limitAgreement with the input reduces this risk. It cannot establish that every reconstructed faint source is real. HST is an observational reference, not an artifact-free image of the physical sky.

Sampling and implementation

Observe — reconstruct — check

A conservative flow learns the structure. A measurement-consistent correction keeps it grounded in the observation.

A field unfolds — one step at a time

  1. Ground observationDESI, seeing-limited. The PSF has already destroyed the high-frequency content, and read noise sits on top of it.
  2. Flow transportThe state starts as pure noise, x⁰ ~ N(0, I). A conditional U-Net carries it along the straight-path interpolant with Euler steps.
  3. Forward projectionThe estimate is pushed back through the forward model A = DsHPSF — a Gaussian PSF of width σPSF = 2, then area downsampling — and the residual against the DESI frame is formed.
  4. Wiener correctionThe residual is Wiener-deconvolved with λSNR = 50 and subtracted, scaled by ηi = η0(1 − i/N). This is what holds hallucinations out.

The flow and its mechanics

Measurement-consistent flow sampling. The velocity network proposes; the forward model disposes. Every step is projected back onto the observation — which is what keeps hallucinated sources out of the estimate.

  1. 1Noisy state xtx⁰ ~ N(0, I)
  2. 2DESI observation ybicubic → HR grid
  3. 3Conditional U-Net vθpixel space · base_dim = 48 · FiLM time conditioning, 256-d
  4. 4Euler stepx̃ = x + vθ Δt
  5. 5Forward projectionA = DsHPSF, σPSF = 2
  6. 6Wiener correctionW(k) = H*(k)/(|H(k)|² + λSNR−1) · residual r = A(x̃) − y
  7. 7Corrected estimatex ← x̃ − ηi F−1(W F[Ds⊤r])

The thumbnails are illustrative stand-ins, not the sampler’s actual intermediate states — step 1 starts from pure Gaussian noise, and the correction runs on residuals, not on Bicubic or HAT images.

05 · Algorithm 1 · MC-FS

Algorithm 1  MC-FS
  inputs   observation y, PSF h, steps N, base step eta_0
  steps    t_i = i / N,   dt = 1 / N
  precompute  W(k) from Eq. (10)
  x <- x0 ~ N(0, I)
  for i = 0 .. N-1 do
    xt  <- x + v_theta(x, t_i, c) * dt     Euler step
    r   <- A(xt) - y                       residual to forward model
    eta_i <- eta_0 * (1 - i / N)           linear decay
    x   <- xt - eta_i * F^-1(W * F[Ds^T r]) Wiener-corrected update
  return x

One sky — two perspectives

Ground-based telescopes are limited by atmospheric seeing, which imposes a stochastic, spatially varying PSF that upsampling alone cannot undo. Space telescopes reach the diffraction limit but see almost none of the sky.

Ground · DESI
1.0–1.3″seeing-limited
Space · HST
~0.09″diffraction-limited
Ratio
11–14×in angular resolution

The 2.4 m HST aperture is diffraction-limited above the atmosphere; DESI is seeing-limited from the ground. The same point source, resolved two ways.

Detail with observational support

Synthetic training pairs do not carry real atmospheric statistics, so a generator learns to please the eye. It produces crisp texture, and occasionally a source that was never in the sky.

  • Regression baselines over-smooth: PSNR rises while structure and flux quietly degrade.
  • Generative baselines invent detail. Pixel metrics reward it; photometry does not.
  • FluxFlow treats an unsupported source as a failure, not a flourish.

What does not. Shear is the flat metric. It is set by the second‑moment tensor of each source, and its orthogonal failure mode is catalog‑level miscentering rather than image quality, so the ranking compresses and the strongest baseline depends on scale and statistic: at ×4 HAT leads both shear mean (0.108) and median (0.081); at ×2 the shear mean is lowest for Bicubic (0.148) and the median for HAT (0.112). FluxFlow lands just behind the leader rather than ahead.

Why the mass median is the headline. The median stellar‑mass MAE is where bright, cleanly segmented sources live; the mean is dragged by a handful of blended or badly segmented objects, which is why GD‑Net posts a competitive mean (7.659) but a median of 1.025.

The paired-image benchmark

DESI–HST is a cross‑survey benchmark: ground‑based seeing‑limited spectra‑era imaging on one side, space‑based diffraction‑limited imaging on the other. Reddy et al. 2024 is also G→S in that table, but without flux calibration and at 2,880 pairs — this set is the only one there combining real data, absolute flux calibration and the ground‑to‑space direction, and the largest real G→S set in it.

DESI · seeing‑limited 1.0–1.3″ FWHM
HST · diffraction‑limited ~0.09″ FWHM

The resolution gap is dominated by atmospheric turbulence, not by pixel scale: a 11–14× ratio in FWHM (a 2.4 m space aperture against DESI's time-varying, spatially extended i‑band seeing). That is what makes DESI→HST super‑resolution substantially harder than benchmarks that rely on pixel‑scale downsampling alone.

Supervised on other benchmarks

The same architecture, with the terms that need flux calibration switched off — the inverse‑variance term, the source‑importance term and the Wiener‑deconvolved measurement consistency are not available on these sets, so an ablated configuration is evaluated instead.

  • STAR at ×2 — highest PSNR and SSIM, together with a top‑tier Flux‑L1.
  • STAR at ×4 — remains competitive with the strongest regression baselines.
  • AstroSR at ×2 — highest PSNR and a competitive SSIM.

The honest caveat. AstroSR is distributed as RGB composites without absolute flux calibration, so total brightness is no longer conserved across the SDSS→HSC mapping. The Flux‑L1 column on that benchmark carries limited physical meaning and is reported only for completeness. The margins over regression baselines are markedly smaller than on DESI–HST, because neither dataset exposes super‑resolution under atmospheric aberration — the dominant degradation mode this method is built for is simply absent.

Why the adjoint is not enough
  1. E.1

    The consistency set

    A source whose morphology has no preimage under A still produces a non‑zero forward residual, so it is penalised wherever the correction enforces y = A(x). Hallucinations are therefore not undetectable in principle — they are trajectories that exit the consistency set C(y), and the whole sampler is built to push them back in.

  2. E.2

    The Wiener kernel from MMSE

    Minimising the mean‑square error over the Fourier‑domain filter, setting the variation to zero and solving gives the optimal filter W(k) = H*(k) / (|H(k)|² + λSNR−1).

  3. E.3

    Spectral analysis

    Naive adjoint back‑projection contracts high‑frequency modes at a rate of |H(k)|² per step, so the loss accumulates into PSF‑scale smoothing across integration steps. Replacing that multiplier with the Wiener kernel keeps the cumulative transfer function stable at high frequencies and preserves the resolution gain the flow provides.

  4. E.4

    Closed‑form toy model

    A normalised 2‑D Gaussian PSF of width σh, probed with a single point source of amplitude α, restricted to the band the LR observation actually supports, iterated for N Euler steps — giving an exact per‑mode recovery factor rather than a fixed‑point approximation.

Two limits and their interpretation

λSNR → ∞

W(k) → H*(k)/|H(k)|² = 1/H(k)

The exact inverse filter. Maximally amplifies high frequencies and is unstable wherever H(k) ≈ 0.

λSNR → 0

W(k) → λSNR H*(k) → 0

The response stays proportional to the matched filter — equivalent, up to scaling, to the raw adjoint. This is exactly the variant the ablation calls w/o Wiener.

λSNR therefore interpolates continuously between the two extremes, and controls how aggressively the deconvolution restores the high‑frequency content the PSF suppressed.

Recovered profile width — FWHM in HR pixels

True PSF 4.71
Naive adjoint · N = 10 6.78
Wiener‑corrected 4.85

At N = 10 the naive recovery is visibly broader than the true PSF — the FWHM is enlarged from 4.71 to 6.78 HR pixels, while the Wiener‑corrected profile comes in at 4.85 and tracks the true PSF with in‑line width. The widening is a low‑pass effect, not noise amplification.

The honest caveat, in the paper's own analysis. If the iteration were run to convergence instead of a finite N, the fixed‑point per‑pixel variance evaluates to roughly 0.21 σ²n at the DESI–HST setting (σh = 2, kmax = π/4). Modest in absolute terms, but it is a substantial amplification of the noise power inside the narrow LR‑supported band, and the prefactor grows exponentially as kmax approaches Nyquist. The practical N = 10 keeps the recovery short of that fixed point — so in this regime the dominant pathology is the finite‑N low‑pass effect of Eq. 27, not the asymptotic noise amplification of Eq. 30.

The one that matters most. Without MC‑FS the sampler produces compact hallucination sources, and the table shows the signature: the highest recall in the whole study (0.553) together with a precision collapse to 0.602. The spurious peaks float as false positives and drag F1 to 0.5256, below the full model's 0.5412.

The quiet trade‑off. w/o WHT posts a marginally better Flux‑L1 (2.814 vs 2.959) — and that is reported rather than hidden, because it comes from fitting drizzle artefacts that overlap the source apertures. Without the WHT term the velocity field treats cosmic‑ray trails and chip gaps as valid targets and reproduces them in the reconstruction; the drops in PSNR, F1 and precision are the real cost.

Source weighting. A uniform loss is dominated by background pixels and cannot preferentially preserve the faint outer galaxy profiles that carry morphological and PSF‑shape information. The two weights are complementary: WHT filters data quality, source weighting emphasises scientifically informative regions.

Latent space. Moving the flow into a frozen SD‑VAE feature space costs 2.33 dB of PSNR and 0.121 of SSIM, and collapses recall to 0.211; the bridge variant that replaces the Gaussian source with a noisy bicubic mean recovers most of the photometry but still trails the full model on precision and F1.

Reading it. FluxFlow trains in 16 h at ×2 and 47 h at ×4 — 2.90% and 8.56% of the cost of the strongest Transformer regression baseline (HAT, 552 h / 549 h) respectively, and at ×4 about 7.7× faster than the heaviest generative baseline (AS‑Bridge, 360 h). Only the two cheapest baselines, cGAN and GD‑Net, train faster at ×2.

Every ConvBlock is Conv2d → LeakyReLU → FiLM → Conv2d → LeakyReLU. The FiLM layer applies a feature‑wise affine modulation conditioned on the time‑conditioning state τ, so the time signal is injected at every resolution rather than only at the input. The timestep t ∈ [0,1] is mapped to τ by a sinusoidal positional embedding followed by a two‑layer MLP of output dimension 256.
